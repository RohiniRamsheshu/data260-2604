Prioritizing Patches: CVSS Score, Exploitability, and the CISA KEV
Catalog
================================================================================

Building on this corpus's dependency-management-guidance and CVSS-
worked-examples entries, this document focuses specifically on the gap
between a vulnerability's theoretical severity score and its practical,
real-world exploitation status, and how organizations reconcile the two
when deciding what to patch first.

The limitation of CVSS base scores for prioritization alone
-------------------------------------------------------------------
CVSS base scores, as explained elsewhere in this corpus, describe a
vulnerability's severity under a reasonably general set of assumptions
about exploitability and impact, but they do not, by design, reflect
whether the vulnerability is actually being exploited by real attackers
at a given point in time. Two vulnerabilities can share an identical
9.8 CVSS score, yet one might have no known public exploit code and no
observed real-world exploitation whatsoever, while the other is being
actively used in ongoing attack campaigns against organizations similar
to one's own -- a distinction the base score alone cannot convey, since
CVSS base scores are computed once, near the time of disclosure, and are
not automatically revised as real-world exploitation evidence emerges
afterward (a separate "temporal" and "environmental" metric group exists
within the CVSS specification to capture exactly this kind of evolving
context, though these optional metric groups are less consistently
published than the base score across most public vulnerability
databases).

The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog
------------------------------------------------------------
The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency maintains a
continuously updated public catalog specifically listing vulnerabilities
with confirmed evidence of active exploitation in the wild, along with a
required remediation deadline for U.S. federal civilian agencies (though
the catalog is used far more broadly, by private-sector organizations
worldwide, as a general prioritization signal rather than only as a
federal compliance requirement). Several vulnerabilities referenced in
this corpus, including Log4Shell and the Struts2 vulnerability
implicated in the Equifax breach, have appeared on this catalog,
reflecting documented, confirmed active exploitation rather than only
theoretical exploitability. Unlike a CVSS score, KEV catalog inclusion is
a binary, evidence-based signal -- a vulnerability is added specifically
because CISA has confirmed real exploitation activity, not because of any
score threshold -- and notably, KEV-listed vulnerabilities span a wide
range of CVSS scores, including some that would not otherwise appear to
be a top remediation priority based on severity score alone.

Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) as a complementary signal
------------------------------------------------------------------------
A separate, related effort, the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS),
maintained by FIRST (the same organization that maintains the CVSS
specification), produces a probabilistic score estimating the likelihood
that a given vulnerability will be exploited in the wild within the next
30 days, based on statistical modeling over historical exploitation and
vulnerability-characteristic data. EPSS is explicitly designed to
complement, rather than replace, CVSS: CVSS answers "how bad would
exploitation be," while EPSS attempts to answer "how likely is
exploitation to actually happen soon," and organizations increasingly
combine both signals (alongside KEV catalog status and asset-specific
context, such as whether the vulnerable component actually faces
untrusted input in a given deployment) to build a more complete
prioritization picture than any single score can provide alone.

A worked prioritization scenario
--------------------------------------
Consider an organization with two open vulnerability findings: one is a
9.8 CVSS-scored issue in an internal batch-processing tool with no
external network exposure and no KEV listing; the other is a 7.5
CVSS-scored issue in a public-facing web application, actively listed on
the CISA KEV catalog with a high EPSS score. Prioritizing purely by raw
CVSS score would rank the first finding as more urgent; incorporating
exploitability context (KEV status, EPSS score, and actual network
exposure) would generally reverse that prioritization, since the second
finding, despite its lower base severity score, represents a
demonstrably more immediate real-world risk given its combination of
active-exploitation evidence and actual reachability from untrusted
input. This kind of reasoning -- combining severity, exploitability
evidence, and deployment-specific context, rather than sorting a
vulnerability backlog by CVSS score alone -- is the general
prioritization approach recommended across most current vulnerability-
management guidance.
