Protestware and Governance Questions in Open-Source Maintainership
=======================================================================

This document expands on two related incidents referenced in this
corpus's npm-supply-chain-history entry -- the colors.js/faker.js
sabotage and the node-ipc "protestware" incident -- to explore the
governance questions they raise, which are distinct from the technical
account-compromise and code-vulnerability questions covered elsewhere in
this corpus.

Distinguishing protestware from a traditional supply-chain attack
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every other supply-chain incident discussed in this corpus involves an
attacker gaining access they were not legitimately entitled to -- whether
through account compromise (ua-parser-js, coa, rc) or an extended,
deceptive trust-building campaign (the XZ Utils backdoor). The colors.js
and node-ipc incidents are structurally different: in both cases, the
person publishing the disruptive or protest-motivated change was the
legitimate, rightful maintainer of the package, exercising what they
would likely describe as their prerogative over their own project, not
someone circumventing any access control at all. This distinction matters
significantly for how the incidents are analyzed and what mitigations are
even conceptually applicable -- two-factor authentication and provenance
signing, discussed in this corpus's maintainer-account-security entry,
address unauthorized access; they do nothing to address a legitimate
maintainer choosing to publish disruptive or harmful code within their
own, rightfully-held authority.

The colors.js/faker.js maintainer's stated motivation
-------------------------------------------------------------
The maintainer of colors and faker published a public statement framing
his actions as protest against what he characterized as large
corporations profiting from his and other unpaid maintainers' free labor
without any compensation or support, after (according to his own
account) becoming frustrated with the broader pattern of commercial
reliance on unpaid open-source infrastructure work. This incident is
widely cited in subsequent discussions of "open-source sustainability" --
the question of how foundational, widely-depended-upon infrastructure
software, often maintained by a single unpaid or under-resourced
individual, should be funded and supported given how much economic
value large organizations derive from depending on it for free.

The node-ipc incident's different ethical framing
--------------------------------------------------------
The node-ipc maintainer's stated motivation was explicitly political --
protest against the Russian invasion of Ukraine -- rather than the
labor-compensation grievance behind the colors.js incident. This
incident drew notably sharper criticism across the open-source community
than the colors.js case, on the grounds that its payload (in one variant,
overwriting arbitrary files on affected systems) was indiscriminate: it
affected any user whose system happened to geolocate to the targeted
countries, regardless of that individual user's own views, actions, or
even nationality (geolocation by IP address is an imprecise signal that
does not reliably correspond to nationality, citizenship, or personal
political responsibility), and regardless of whether the affected system
belonged to an ordinary individual developer or ran in a context where
data loss could cause serious real-world harm.

Package registries' evolving response
------------------------------------------
Following both incidents, npm removed the affected malicious versions
and, in later cases, has taken a more active stance discouraging
"protestware" broadly in its terms of service, treating deliberately
harmful payloads as a policy violation regardless of the publishing
maintainer's underlying motivation or legitimate authority over the
package. This reflects a governance position that a package's downstream
consumers are entitled to assume a published version will not
deliberately harm them, as a baseline expectation independent of
whatever grievance, however legitimate in the abstract, a maintainer
might hold.

Why this matters for dependency risk assessment
-------------------------------------------------------
For organizations assessing dependency risk, protestware incidents
introduce a risk category that traditional vulnerability scanning
(oriented around detecting known code-level flaws or known-malicious
package versions) does not straightforwardly anticipate: a package can
be entirely free of known vulnerabilities and have no history of
account-compromise incidents, while still carrying a nonzero risk that
its legitimate maintainer chooses, at some future point, to publish a
deliberately disruptive version. This is one of several reasons the
dependency-management-guidance entry in this corpus recommends pinning
and controlled, deliberate version updates (rather than automatically
accepting the newest published version of every dependency) as a general
practice, since it provides at least some window to detect and evaluate
a newly-published version, from any cause, before it is automatically
deployed into production systems.

the colors.js and node-ipc incidents, npm registry policy updates
