Case Study: The 2017 Equifax Data Breach and CVE-2017-5638
================================================================

This document provides an extended case study of the 2017 Equifax data
breach, since it remains one of the most thoroughly investigated and
publicly documented examples of how a known, patched open-source package
vulnerability translated into a real-world large-scale data breach,
offering concrete lessons for the "why patching matters" question that
often accompanies discussion of individual CVEs.

Background: the vulnerable component
------------------------------------------
Equifax, one of the three major U.S. consumer credit reporting agencies,
operated a web portal (an online dispute-filing system for consumers)
built using Apache Struts 2, a Java web application framework. As detailed
in this corpus's dedicated Struts2 entry, Struts 2 versions before 2.3.32
and 2.5.10.1 contained CVE-2017-5638, a critical remote-code-execution
vulnerability in the framework's file-upload handling, triggerable via a
crafted Content-Type HTTP header with no authentication required.

Timeline
--------------
Apache Struts publicly disclosed CVE-2017-5638 on March 7, 2017, alongside
the release of patched versions 2.3.32 and 2.5.10.1. The U.S. Computer
Emergency Readiness Team (US-CERT) issued a corresponding alert the same
week, and the vulnerability received widespread security-industry
coverage given its maximum CVSS severity score and trivial
exploitability. According to Equifax's own subsequent disclosures and the
U.S. Government Accountability Office's later investigative report,
attackers began exploiting the still-unpatched vulnerability in Equifax's
systems on May 13, 2017 -- more than two months after the public
disclosure and patch availability. The intrusion continued undetected
until July 29, 2017, when Equifax's security team identified anomalous
network traffic and began an internal investigation. Equifax publicly
disclosed the breach on September 7, 2017.

Scale of impact
--------------------
The breach ultimately exposed the personal information of approximately
147 million people, primarily U.S. consumers, including full names,
Social Security numbers, birth dates, addresses, and, for a smaller subset
of affected individuals, driver's license numbers and credit card
numbers. Given that Social Security numbers are used across the U.S.
financial system as a persistent identity credential that cannot easily be
"reissued" the way a credit card number can, the breach was widely
characterized as having long-term identity-theft risk implications for
the vast majority of the affected U.S. adult population.

Why the patch was not applied in time
-------------------------------------------
Equifax's own public statements and the subsequent GAO investigation
identified multiple contributing organizational failures rather than a
single root cause: an internal vulnerability-scanning process that failed
to correctly identify all systems running the vulnerable Struts version;
a since-expired SSL/TLS inspection certificate that had caused a network
monitoring device to stop decrypting and inspecting a category of traffic
for an extended period prior to the breach, delaying detection even after
the intrusion began; and, more broadly, an acknowledged gap between the
existence of a documented, publicly available patch and the organization's
actual ability to identify every affected system and apply that patch
across its infrastructure within the two-month exploitation window.

Broader lessons for vulnerability management
--------------------------------------------------
The Equifax case is frequently cited in vulnerability-management training
and policy discussions for several specific, generalizable lessons: (a) a
patch being publicly available does not equal a patch being applied --
organizational and process gaps between disclosure and actual remediation
represent a distinct risk surface in their own right, separate from the
underlying code vulnerability; (b) accurate asset inventory (knowing
precisely which systems run which software, including transitive
framework dependencies) is a prerequisite for effective patching at scale,
since an organization cannot patch a vulnerable component it does not know
it has; and (c) detection capability matters as a compensating control
even when patching lags, since the roughly eleven-week gap between initial
exploitation and detection in this case significantly increased the
ultimate scope of data exposed, compared to a scenario with faster
detection even under the same delayed-patch conditions.
